resisting big tech through learning

15th October 2026. From a train at 6am.

Let’s talk about what web 3.0.

The optimism of the world wide web that Sir Tim Berners Lee spun onto the world was a moment of joy. Watching it grow. Spinning my own lines. Connecting to others. It seemed, in the early 90s, to be a place for curiosity and shared learning. The first web page I made was for Stephen Hawking in 1994. People connected, ideas grew, new practices were born. Then came social media to fill the web with junk. People became increasingly divided. Advertising made everyone search for gold. Influences spun the gold of the web into their own threads. Sticky threads. Made to lure you in. To trap. Shared learning became shared unlearning. I said. He said. She said. I said. The greedy just wanted more and more. The world wide web and everything it stood for has been made sticky and greedy by just a handful of companies that have, in the words of the very brilliant Cory Doctorow, turned the web into a “big pile of shit”.

But it doesn’t have to be like that. Below the stinking mess the web is still there. We just need to reclaim it. To bring back that optimism. To bring back shared learning. To bring back a sense of community.

But wait, you’re just talking another cocky David Vs Goliath story. Not at all dear reader. Hold on for a minute, I’m going to add a side-bar twist of good old fashioned kitchen tale in here. I cook. A lot. It defines me. When I’m cooking I’m not cooking in anger. I’m cooking because I love food. I love making things that I and others can eat. Nobody says of me when I’m cooking up a storm in my kitchen “hold on there buddy, there’s no point you cooking with all them there fresh vegetables and the likes - you’ll never take down the fast food industry”. While I (mostly) reject unhealthy food, I am not cooking as an act of resistance. I’m just making food that I like. That nourishes me. That I try to learn what foods work. To learn ways of cooking not taught to me. Not passed down from my parents or grandparents (all actually pretty bad cooks… sorry mum, dad, grandpa, nanna, granny… you did your best). I’m not going to romanticise their cooking. It wasn’t great. I learnt that early and started to cook for myself as soon as I could. As I was learning to cook, I was also learning to code and express myself. I like writing code and making things. But I don’t like that unlike many ways you can access your own foods (right down to the seeds to grow your own), we have nothing like that in Big Tech. We are locked out. Everyone can grow there own food and connect it to a system of community gardens, farmers’ markets, roadside venders and local shops. I grow chillies in pots on my windowsill. I grow potatoes and pumpkins and broad beans (yes I LOVE broad beans) on my community garden. I buy food from supermarkets. I buy food from unhealthy takeaways. The point being - it’s my choice. Ok, I think you get my point. There needs to be parity across sectors.

I feel if you’ve come this far in reading, then you’ll indulge a bit of a trip through my memory neurons.

Here’s my origin story.

I have tasted power. Real power. The kind of power that I guess drives Big Tech Bros. Jon Rogers aged 11 was given a ZX Spectrum computer. I was driving my parents (and large family - I’m the middle of five) nuts. Asthmatic. Filled with steroids. Scratchy. Wheezy. Weedy. Allergic to everything. My teacher (the very wonderful Mrs Mason of Benson Primary School) described me as a bottle of fizzy pop (that’s juice in scotland and soda in the US) that was shaken up and nobody could get the lid off. I’m still that person. If you know me, you’ll know. But that ZX spectrum, it focused me in ways nothing (except cooking) could. It was a computer you plugged into the TV and you wrote code and graphics and sounds leap out of the screen. Your graphics. Your sounds. But on the family TV. The ONLY family TV. In 1982 there were two content providers for our TV. The BBC and ITV. But on that day in 1982 everything changed for the Rogers household. The TV had three content providers. The BBC, ITV and me. “Mum Jon’s doing weird shit with the TV again”. “Dad I want to watch, but Jon’s typing at it”. “Mum Jon’s got the TV saying “Fuck off!”... and so on. the POWER. O to the M to the G. It felt good people. Weedy, scratchy, wheezy Jon - the power in my hands. “Ah but mum and dad I’m learning….”. And I did. What a thing of joy. A thing of power. Is this what a pen felt like in the hands of Shakespeare?

Then came electronics. You could make your own things that connected to the TV. By then it was a dedicated display. A midi controller. My casio electronic piano could be played by a file on my computer. Big Track hacked. Later, at uni, a robot arm to pick up concrete slabs (all those summers at Rogers Concrete). Making making making. Connecting. Building. Learning.

Then came the web. The coffee pot at Cambridge Computer Science Lab that we could watch daily. Neural networks. Making things start to learn. A switch to teaching electronics at the Royal College of Art. Rethinking everything you could ever think about a computer. Gillian Crampton Smith , Rory Hamilton, Bill Gaver, Fiona Raby, Tony Dunne. Thinking in ways I’d never seen in engineering school. Thinking through making. Making through thinking. The optimism. The web. Things. Infinite possibilities. My career as a creative tech lit up. Given life. The future was multiple coloured and looking pretty damn good from my lofty position as a gen X Phd student.

It wasn’t until many years later walking through one of the connecting tunnels on London's underground with Michcelle Thorne, that something dawned on me. It was that pivot. I’d joined her as a senior Fellow with Mozilla in 2016. The web and everything that the internet stood for had gone to shit. Surveillance capitalism was thriving. Manipulating us. The rich getting richer. Their power, more powerful. All of those years teaching the opportunities afforded by the internet. Was I wrong? Had I swallowed the cool aid of tech. Was I the problem?

The answer is simplest in German. Jein!.1 Ja, opportunities are important. Why be a lecturer or professor if this isn’t about generating opportunities for our students through learning. But, and here’s the “nein”, it was becoming clear that it was reckless to teach and research opportunities without countering that with responsibilities. Opportunities and responsibilities. A cycle. A learning cycle. There is no internet without responsibilities. There are no opportunities without responsibilities.

Then came large language model AI. The hungry caterpillar of all computing. A very hungry caterpillar. Eating and eating. On day One it ate one apple. On day Two it ate the whole of the world wide web. All of it. And what Eric Carle didn’t tell the millions of parents that read that book to children, was just how much shit that caterpillar must have given out. The very hungry LLM it’s shit is everywhere. It’s suffocating the web. Killing it. Search - gone. Shared learning - gone. Optimism - gone. Covered in dis- and misinformed- excrement. There is no community. Video did kill the radio star.

But stop right there Mr Me.

All is not lost! It’s now ours to reclaim. And the point I’m trying to draw the dots to. Let’s just start to create webpages. To buy up domain names (as David Chatting yesterday declared) like it’s 2005! To find green ways to host content. Tread lightly on the web. To find ways to express ourselves again. To connect. Form community. Reclaim the web - not as David fighting Goliath but as a third space. Our space. That’s why I’ve made this site. After years of trying different ways to engage and avoid. It’s time to come back and contribute. Let’s find our Web 3.0. As a third space between Big Tech and despair. To find where our hope lives. Just a few connections at a time. Thank you Sir Tim Berners Lee - we are back!
